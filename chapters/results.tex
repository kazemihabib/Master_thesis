\chapter{Results and Discussion}
\label{chap:results}

This chapter presents the empirical evaluation and forensic analysis of multimodal large language models for single image face swap detection. Across all experiments, models are evaluated in off the shelf configurations without parameter fine tuning, measuring their intrinsic ability to identify facial manipulation artifacts under zero shot and few shot prompting regimes.

% -------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Overview and Experimental Roadmap}
\label{sec:results_overview}

To manage computational tractability, experiments follow an integrated two tier structure within each stage. Experiments are first systematically explored and filtered on balanced development sets from FOWS and GOTCHA. The optimal configurations identified in each stage are then validated on the complete benchmark test partitions, comprising over \num{80000} frames across both datasets.

\section{Stage 1: Zero Shot Lexical Search and Model Selection}
\label{sec:results_stage1}

Stage 1 evaluates the intrinsic zero shot capability of all nine candidate models across the complete $4 \times 7 = 28$ grid of real and fake target word pairs on the balanced development sets of FOWS and GOTCHA. The objective of this initial stage is threefold:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Assess the baseline feasibility of zero shot face swap detection across diverse model architectures;
    \item Quantify model sensitivity to target class vocabulary words; and
    \item Identify the optimal word pair for each model and select the top five performers to advance to Stage~2.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Instruction Following and Parsing Reliability}
\label{subsec:stage1_reliability}

Across all nine models, 28 prompt word pairs, and two development sets, a total of \num{677376} individual image classifications were evaluated. Under the deterministic single phrase extraction policy ($\pi(t)$, \Cref{sec:formulation}), eight of the nine evaluated models achieved a \num{100}\% parse success rate with zero formatting errors. Across the entire evaluation, only a single query from Nemotron-Nano-12B failed to emit a clean binary verdict. The strict single phrase format constraint was followed reliably across all model architectures.

\subsection{Model Specific Vocabulary Preferences}
\label{subsec:stage1_vocab_preferences}

A central finding of Stage 1 is that \textbf{there is no universal optimal word pair} for zero shot face swap detection across multimodal architectures. As reported in \Cref{tab:results_stage1_winners}, every one of the top five advancing models achieves its highest MCC on a completely distinct vocabulary pair:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Qwen3.6-35B-A3B} performs best with \texttt{authentic} / \texttt{faceswapped};
    \item \textbf{Qwen3.6-27B} achieves its peak with \texttt{bona fide} / \texttt{faceswapped};
    \item \textbf{gemma-4-31B-it} prefers \texttt{bona fide} / \texttt{generated};
    \item \textbf{Kimi-VL-A3B-Instruct} reaches its highest score with \texttt{original} / \texttt{synthetic}; and
    \item \textbf{Llama-4-Scout-17B} favors \texttt{authentic} / \texttt{fake}.
\end{itemize}

This divergence aligns with prior findings showing that multimodal models have distinct, model specific lexical sensitivities~\cite{dumpala2024sensitivity,ismithdeen2025promptception}. Evaluating all models with a single default pair (such as \texttt{real} / \texttt{fake}) would unfairly penalize models that prefer other words, underestimating their true detection ability.

\begin{enumerate}

\subsection{Selection of Advancing Models}
\label{subsec:stage1_selection}

Based on the development set results, the five top ranking models (Qwen3.6-35B-A3B, Qwen3.6-27B, gemma-4-31B-it, Kimi-VL-A3B-Instruct, and Llama-4-Scout-17B) are selected to advance to Stage~2. Each selected model is strictly locked to its own Stage~1 winning vocabulary pair (\Cref{tab:results_stage1_winners}) for all subsequent structural and persona evaluations.

The remaining four models (Nemotron-Nano-12B, GLM-4.6V-Flash, InternVL3.5-30B, and Phi-4-multimodal) achieved peak scores below \num{0.380} overall MCC on the development sets and were excluded from further exploration to maintain computational tractability.

\section{Stage 2: Structural Framing and Persona Engineering}
\label{sec:results_stage2}

Building upon the locked winning vocabulary pairs from Stage 1, Stage 2 evaluates how semantic definitions, task descriptions, and expert personas affect zero shot face swap detection. Following the taxonomy of nine structural templates defined in \Cref{tab:stage2_structures}, the five advancing models (\texttt{Qwen3.6-35B-A3B}, \texttt{Qwen3.6-27B}, \texttt{gemma-4-31B-it}, \texttt{Kimi-VL-A3B-Instruct}, and \texttt{Llama-4-Scout-17B}) are evaluated across 34 prompt configurations on the balanced development sets.

\subsection{Overall Structural and Persona Impact}
\label{subsec:stage2_overall}

\Cref{fig:stage2_overall_whiskers} illustrates the overall performance change ($\Delta\text{MCC}_{\text{overall}}$) relative to each model's locked Stage 1 baseline across the evaluated configurations:

\begin{figure}[t]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/stage2_overall_whiskers.png}
	\caption{Overall MCC change ($\Delta\text{MCC}_{\text{overall}}$) relative to the Stage 1 baseline on the balanced development sets across all nine structural templates. Colored bars represent explanation prompts without personas (templates 1--4), whiskers show the score range across the six expert personas (templates 6--9), gray markers denote persona only prompts (template 5), and white dots mark persona means.}
	\label{fig:stage2_overall_whiskers}
\end{figure}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Explanation Effects and Persona Alignment:} For four of the five models (\texttt{Qwen3.6-35B-A3B}, \texttt{Qwen3.6-27B}, \texttt{Kimi-VL-A3B-Instruct}, and \texttt{Llama-4-Scout-17B}), adding semantic definitions or task descriptions alone consistently reduces detection performance relative to the Stage 1 baseline, with drops reaching up to \num{0.51} $\Delta\text{MCC}$. Only \texttt{gemma-4-31B-it} benefits directly from standalone explanations (\texttt{def\_sys} improves overall MCC by \num{+0.163}, and \texttt{task\_sys} by \num{+0.153}). For Qwen35 and Qwen27, improvements over the baseline are unlocked only when combining these definitions and tasks with an aligned expert persona (for example, \texttt{P6} Content Integrity Moderator on Qwen35). In contrast, for \texttt{Kimi-VL-A3B-Instruct} and \texttt{Llama-4-Scout-17B}, the extra context consistently hurts performance, yielding no meaningful improvement over their Stage 1 baselines regardless of structural placement or persona choice. Furthermore, persona choice is critical: while aligned personas restore and enhance performance, unsuitable personas degrade scores even further.
    \item \textbf{System vs User Role Placement:} The impact of placing explanations in the system message versus the user message is not uniform across model architectures or explanation types:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item In \texttt{gemma-4-31B-it}, system message placement is consistently superior across both types, yielding large gains on \texttt{def\_sys} (\num{+0.163} vs \num{-0.123} on \texttt{def\_usr}) and on \texttt{task\_sys} (\num{+0.154} vs \num{+0.008} on \texttt{task\_usr}).
        \item In \texttt{Qwen3.6-35B-A3B}, the preferred placement depends on the explanation type: for semantic definitions, system placement is less harmful than user placement (\num{-0.247} on \texttt{def\_sys} vs \num{-0.280} on \texttt{def\_usr}), whereas for task descriptions, user placement is less harmful (\num{-0.081} on \texttt{task\_usr} vs \num{-0.103} on \texttt{task\_sys}).
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

The full benchmark evaluation highlights several key findings:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Gemma Achieves Top Rank on Full Benchmark:} On the complete test partitions, \texttt{gemma-4-31B-it} emerges as the top performing model overall (\num{0.609} MCC), surpassing both Qwen architectures. This advantage is driven by Gemma's stronger performance on FOWS (\num{0.526} vs \num{0.422} for Qwen35 and \num{0.388} for Qwen27).
    \item \textbf{GOTCHA Robustness Across Scale:} On GOTCHA, all three models maintain high performance when moving from the development sample to the full dataset. \texttt{Qwen3.6-35B-A3B} and \texttt{Qwen3.6-27B} score \num{0.767} and \num{0.752} respectively, while \texttt{gemma-4-31B-it} increases from \num{0.643} on the development set to \num{0.692} on the full benchmark.
    \item \textbf{FOWS Performance on Full Partitions:} On FOWS, all three models achieve lower MCC scores on the full test partition compared to their development set results.
    \item \textbf{Parse Reliability at Scale:} The deterministic single phrase output constraint was followed reliably. Both Qwen models recorded zero parse failures across all queries, while \texttt{gemma-4-31B-it} produced only a single unparseable response (\emph{"Unable to determine"}).
    \item \textbf{Consistent Occlusion Behavior at Scale:} Occlusion dynamics on the full test partitions mirror the development set results: all three models on GOTCHA achieve higher detection performance on occluded frames (placing all models below the diagonal), while on FOWS each model maintains its relative position, with \texttt{gemma-4-31B-it} and \texttt{Qwen3.6-35B-A3B} scoring higher on occluded frames and \texttt{Qwen3.6-27B} scoring higher on non occluded frames.
\end{itemize}
